The Ramayana – An Historical Study.*

BY

MR. P. V. NANU PILLAI, B.A.

Of all the great literary productions of ancient India none is more widely popular or is more generally appreciated by Hindus of all classes and of different degrees of intelligence than the Ramayana of Valmiki. There are greater and even sublimer works, such as the Vedas and the Upanishads. But none of them except the Mahabharatha can be said to be so well-known to and appreciated by the people at large. Even the Mahabharatha is not so much an epic poem, as it is an encyclopædia of ancient Hindu legends and folklore, collected and collated by successive authors known by the general designation of Vyasa. Further it is an unwieldy work, perhaps the longest poetical work in the World. The Ramayana, on the other hand, is the work of a single author, Valmiki, justly known as the Adikavi or the first poet. He is not merely first in the order of time, but first also in the order of merit. Of all the epic poems in the world, the Ramayana stands unparalleled. Its peculiar fascination lies in the compactness of the story, in the chasteness and the simplicity of its language, in the loftiness of its ethics and above all in the religious instruction which it imparts to its votaries. The word Rama is a household name in every Hindu family. It has such significance to the Hindu mind that it is almost synonymous with the name of God—so much so that the very uttering of the word, a number of times, constitutes a round of prayer in the case of an ordinary Hindu. The word Rama is derived from the word ram which means to please, and it is used in the sense of Atmaram, i.e., that which pleases the soul.
